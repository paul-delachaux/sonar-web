title: "In Absentia "
description: >
  Today we delve into the abysses of the human mind, where the purest beauty
  meets the violence and darkness of the depths. For this very first edition of
  Sound of the Sonar, we’re turning on the radars and diving deep into a
  masterpiece of modern progressive rock : Porcupine Tree’s hypnotic and
  chilling album In Absentia.


  Porcupine Tree merely started off as a joke, it was an imaginary band created by Steven Wilson behind which he could release his own music. After meeting some initial success, he recruited other musicians to form a proper band. Porcupine Tree's musical journey could be broken down into three eras : starting with the psychedelic era, evolving into more pop-rock oriented songwriting, and finally embracing a heavier metal sound. In Absentia is often considered a turning point in the band’s career. During that time, Steven Wilson was getting more into heavy music and started producing for the metal band Opeth. This motivated him to incorporate more of these elements into his sound. In Absentia marks as well, and above all, the arrival of Gavin Harrison who is replacing the band's former drummer Chris Maitland.


  What really sets this record apart is the combination of the metal influence with his former psychedelic and pop sensibilities. Indeed, the record is very diverse : some soft tracks (Trains, Prodigal), some heavy ones (Wedding Nails, Strip The Soul) or both (Blackest Eyes, Gravity Eyelids), as well as some atmospheric tunes (Lips of Ashes, .3).


  On the pop side, the album delivers catchy hooks, sweet melodies, and Wilson's soft vocals. On the metal side, it unleashes distorted and layered guitars, as well as powerful and technical drumming, featuring occasional double-kick fills. Finally, the psychedelic side weaves in trippy instrumental sections, atmospheric soundscapes, a variety of audio effects, and hypnotic, repetitive basslines. All of these influences are blended in the record without any friction.


  One of the record's strengths is the impeccable pacing of compositions and the seamless transitions. 


  Every single track justifies its runtime, it never feels like the tracks are dragging on too long or that they’ve been rushed. They are all given enough time to build a beautifully intense atmosphere, but are not overstaying their welcome.


  Moving beyond the simple type of verse/chorus layouts, these tracks feature elaborated song structures that serve the emotional purpose of the music. The seamless transitions bridge the different sections naturally, allowing the tracks to play with the listener’s expectations and resolve in a compelling way.


  The record achieves a great balance between all of its aspects. It remains accessible whilst being a prog record. There are a handful of odd time signatures and rhythmic switch ups happening, yet done very smoothly, and the complex arrangements are not distracting from the essence of the music. This balance is also reflected in the dynamic of the tracks, ranging from quiet to nervous and creating a sense of journey throughout the album.


  If the songwriting of this album is nothing short of incredible by itself, the sound design is what takes the music to a whole other level. The arrangements that are offered to us add a lot more to the typical guitar-bass-drums line up, with sparks of pianos and acoustic guitars, not to mention the addition of atypical instruments like a Dulcimer in Lips of Ashes, and a banjo in Trains. In addition to that, and like often in Prog, there are plenty of synths parts and occasional orchestral arrangements such as the string sections on .3 and Collapse the Light Into Earth.


  Richard Barbieri’s synth work on this record is really masterful, it adds so much depth, helps to build a strong atmosphere and is always blending in with the other instruments. The symbiosis between electronics/ambients and traditional instrumentation is flawless, Gravity Eyelids is a great example of that. I also love how the soundscape sometimes opens up with the addition of vocal harmonies and panned acoustic guitar strumming, like in the chorus of Sound of Muzak or Prodigal. As for the percussions, the drumming in the album is always on point, very musical, almost melodic with a lot of cymbals playing. Gavin Harrison is dressing up all his parts with little details that gives the music so much life and groove. The intricacy and flexibility of his playing style is impressive. Let’s not forget about Colin Edwin’s very tasteful bass playing, a performance full of restraint like in Sound of Muzak where he only plays during the chorus. Moreover, he has some killer lines, such as the one in Strip The Soul.


  The music boasts an incredible level of detail, characterized by meticulously crafted, subtle textures that continuously surface throughout each track. This rich soundscape gives your ears something to feed upon at all times without ever feeling overloaded. This gives the album a great relisten value, as there is always something new to discover.


  The production and sound engineering of the record are absolutely pristine, everything sounds wonderful. Each musical element occupies its own untroubled expanse, breathing in perfect harmony without ever interfering with the others. Nothing takes you out of the music, the experience is very immersive.


  In Absentia’s emotional landscape shifts inside the spectrum of a sickened mind : from wistful nostalgia and tense, sedative lulls to isolation, insanity and outbursts of violence. While it eschews the traditional narrative of a concept album, there’s a dark, psychological thread binding most of the tracks together. The collection acts as a descent into the fractured human psyche, exploring the hollow expanses of mental illness, alienation, and the bleak emptiness that defines the minds of serial killers. The opener of the album Blackest Eyes announces right away the kind of dichotomy between mellow tunes and aggressive tones interspersed with ambient sections that we can find throughout the record.


  In fact the song intertwines all of those aspects : the atmospheric intro is reiterated later during the bridge but as a very heavy riff, and, amidst the ballad-like verses, we can pick up creepy whispers hinting towards the psychosis of the character of the song.

  The distorted opening riff functions as an overture : a cinematic curtain-raiser signaling the tragic birth of a protagonist. From this initial burst of trauma, the character is steadily drawn toward the precarious edge of sanity.


  Some songs on the album do not directly fit into this narrative though, for instance Trains which is a lot more about nostalgia.  Steven used to hear trains passing through from his bedroom, so in his psyche trains are attached to childhood and more innocent times. On this track he laments how the good times of that era slept away from him too fast.


  Sound of Muzak elegises the quiet devaluation of music. Steven is drawing a parallel between the music industry and Muzak, an asepticized and normalised acoustic wallpaper engineered for the sterile corridors of commercial infrastructures, from humming elevators to sprawling shopping malls and airports. Muzak traces its origins to the eponymous company founded in the 1930s. Its initial aim was to improve workers’ productivity and increase consumers’ willingness to buy.


  The purpose of this track is to mourn a cultural shift, the transformation of music from a vessel of artistic expression into a consumer product. This critique feels remarkably clairvoyant, anticipating the hyper-commodified landscape of the streaming era, as we remind you this album came out in 2002, years before Spotify established itself. What makes the song so compelling to me is that it succeeds in being catchy as probably the most commercial song of the album, while preserving a real musical appeal with for instance the use of a 7/4 time signature in the verses, which I find ironic.


  Next we have Heartattack in a Layby, a song about the last thoughts of a man dying on the side of the road, not realising that he’ll never find his way back home. This track is more minimalistic compared to others, really focusing on setting up the scene for the devastating story it tells. Somehow the acoustic guitar of the chorus makes you understand that something tragic is happening.


  Born in the aftermath of the 9/11/2001 events, the closer track Collapse the Light Into Earth stands as a haunting meditation on grief. The song is utterly sublime, drawing the album to a closure in a delicate interplay between light and shadow, reminiscent of a sunset (hence the title). This is not a happy ending, it’s bitter but hopeful at the same time, similar to when one loses something forever but lies in a state of acceptance.


  As for the other songs, they’re expanding more on insanity, worsening as the album goes by.

  Lips of Ashes evokes a profound sense of social alienation, a rupture of human connection where the ability to reach another soul has withered away, leaving the protagonist to sink slowly into a state of apathy.

  The track captures these emotions with a spellbinding, breath-taking grace, evoking the disorienting stillness of drifting through deep space or being submerged beneath an endless ocean.


  Gravity Eyelids carries on the mesmerizing setting established in Lips Of Ashes, but here the vulnerability of the previous song is swapped with a sedated and tense atmosphere, as if the protagonist is losing touch with reality and has become emotionally numb. This track for me is the heart of In Absentia, it’s the one that matches the album cover and conveys that feeling of absence the most, where the protagonist reaches the point of no return.


  The song is slowly building up, starting off with mellotron choirs and a trip-hopish drumbeat, giving it an underground and ominous vibe. The song becomes eerier by the minute, with completely stoned vocals and mystical sounding keys. Then, about halfway through the track, there's a teasing towards heaviness, followed by a drop of distorted guitars and a wild synth lead. It’s a strike of violence, probably illustrating the protagonist’s act of defiance, finally releasing his contained madness.


  Serving as a bridge of escalating tension, the instrumental Wedding Nails remains my least favored piece on the record. Grounded in an opening riff that feels overly familiar, less an original statement and more a recycled echo of classic motifs like Deep Purple’s Rat Bat Blue or Led Zeppelin’s Moby Dick which were already sampled from Bobby Parker’s Watch Your Step. Still it’s a very fine jam showcasing the great musicianship of the band.


  Opening the album’s second half, Prodigal, reintroduces the wistful, nostalgic warmth first felt on Trains. Softened by the weeping tones of a slide guitar, the track slows the record's pace, offering a gentler acoustic sanctuary. Yet beneath this soothing exterior lies a stark thematic contrast : the lyrics portray a man paralyzed by emotional vacancy and depression, hopelessly adrift in his own darkening psyche.


  The subsequent track, .3, unfolds as an otherworldly instrumental space where psychedelic textures and a lush string orchestra intertwine. The piece offers a suspended interlude on the record, an auditory ascent. Yet within this ethereal atmosphere lies a sensation of numbness, perfectly mirroring the cold, mechanical mantra of war industrialization echoed throughout the bridge, which follows a momentary industrial drum beat further reinforcing this idea.


  The Creator Has a Mastertape is a breathless, chaotic descent into mania. The track captures a deeply unhinged protagonist who has surrendered all psychological restraint, abandoning himself entirely to his most violent and macabre impulses.

  The song does a great job at capturing the frantic madness of the mind of a serial killer, it sounds like an unleashed beast, completely beyond the reach of control.


  By now we’ve arrived at the final stage of human insanity, Strip the Soul closes this trend of brutality with a climax. Here the protagonist thrives on the different abuses he is inflicting to others, always delving deeper into violence, ultimately becoming a killing machine. Like a black hole getting bigger as it absorbs matter, his hole needs ever more killing to be filled. The outro of the song is absolutely crushing, reflecting the insatiable appetite for destruction of the protagonist.


  Ultimately, In Absentia stands as a milestone in Porcupine Tree’s discography. Seamlessly weaving heavy metal riffs, pop melodies and atmospheric psychedelia into a large scaled cohesive masterpiece. 


  Driven by a sought out sound design, intricate arrangements and immaculate production, the album transforms dark psychological themes into a sonic journey. It balances visceral intensity with venerable beauty, proving that intricate progressive music can remain accessible and emotionally resonant. 


  Whether you are a seasoned progressive rock fan or a curious newcomer, In Absentia is an essential immersive listening experience that deserves to be heard from start to finish.
